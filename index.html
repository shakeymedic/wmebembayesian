Two Lenses for Uncertainty

A Guide to Bayesian & Frequentist Reasoning in Medicine

Core Philosophies

The Frequentist Framework

Views probability as the long-run frequency of an event over infinite, repeated trials. It's the bedrock of most modern medical evidence.

Analogy: The probability of a coin toss being 'heads' is 50% because, over millions of tosses, the proportion would converge to 0.5.

Parameters are Fixed: The 'true' effect of a drug is a single, unknown constant. The data we collect is random.

Key Tools: P-values and Confidence Intervals are used to test a pre-defined 'Null Hypothesis' (e.g., "the drug has no effect").

The Bayesian Paradigm

Views probability as a degree of belief in a proposition. This belief is updated as new evidence becomes available.

Analogy: A clinician's initial suspicion for a diagnosis (prior belief) is updated by a new test result (evidence), leading to a revised diagnosis (posterior belief).

Parameters are Random: The unknown drug effect is treated as a variable with a probability distribution representing our uncertainty about it.

Key Tools: Bayes' Theorem, Credible Intervals, and Bayes Factors are used to update beliefs and compare hypotheses.

The Engine Room: Key Mechanics

Null Hypothesis Significance Testing (NHST)

A formal procedure assuming "no effect" (H₀) and testing if the data are surprising enough to reject that assumption.

P-value: A Measure of Surprise

The probability of getting your result (or more extreme) IF the null hypothesis is true. A small p-value (e.g., < 0.05) means the data is surprising, providing evidence against the null.

Common Pitfall: The p-value is NOT the probability that the null hypothesis is true.

95% Confidence Interval (CI): A Range for the Truth

A range of plausible values for the true parameter. The "95% confidence" is a property of the method, not the specific interval.

Correct Interpretation: "If we repeated this study many times, 95% of the CIs calculated would contain the true value." It's about the long-run performance of the procedure.

Bayes' Theorem: The Engine of Learning

Posterior ∝ Likelihood × Prior

Updated Belief = Evidence from Data × Initial Belief

95% Credible Interval: An Intuitive Range

A range of values for the parameter with a direct probabilistic meaning.

Correct Interpretation: "Given our data and model, there is a 95% probability that the true value lies within this interval." This is what most people *think* a confidence interval means.

Bayes Factor (BF): Weighing the Evidence

Directly compares how well two competing hypotheses predict the data. A BF₁₀ of 15 means the data are 15 times more likely under the alternative hypothesis (H₁) than the null (H₀). Crucially, it can also provide evidence *for* the null.

At a Glance: Core Differences

Feature Frequentist Approach Bayesian Approach
Definition of Probability Long-run frequency of an event; objective property of the world. Degree of belief in a proposition; subjective property of an observer's knowledge.
Nature of Parameters Fixed, unknown constants. Cannot make probability statements about them. Random variables with probability distributions representing uncertainty.
Primary Question "What is P(Data | H₀)?" (Probability of the data, assuming no effect) "What is P(H₁ | Data)?" (Probability of the hypothesis, given the data)
Interval Interpretation Confidence Interval: If repeated, 95% of intervals would contain the true value. Credible Interval: There is a 95% probability the true value lies in this specific interval.
Prior Information Not formally incorporated into calculations. Formally incorporated via the Prior distribution.
Ability to Support the Null Cannot prove the null. Can only "fail to reject" it. Can provide evidence for the null via Bayes Factors.

The Bayesian Mind in the Emergency Department

Clinical judgement is an intuitive act of Bayesian updating. Formalising it can reduce diagnostic error.

Interactive Case Study: Diagnosing Pulmonary Embolism

Let's walk through a case using formal Bayesian reasoning to see how our belief (probability) shifts with new evidence.

Step 1: Establish the Prior (Pre-Test Probability)

A 55-year-old male with pleuritic chest pain and tachycardia. Wells' score stratifies him into the Moderate Risk category.

Pre-Test Probability (PTP) of PE: 20%

Step 2: Incorporate Evidence (D-dimer)

The D-dimer test comes back Positive. A positive result in a moderate-risk patient has a Likelihood Ratio (LR) of ~2.0, increasing our suspicion.

Step 3: Definitive Imaging (CTPA)

The diagnosis is still uncertain, so we proceed to CTPA. The scan is Positive for a segmental PE. This has a very high LR of ~20.0.

Towards a Unified Statistical Literacy

The debate isn't about one framework defeating the other. It's about becoming "bilingual" — understanding the right tool for the right question.

Frequentist methods remain the standard for large, confirmatory trials where controlling long-run error rates for regulatory approval is paramount.
Bayesian methods excel in adaptive trials, rare diseases, and formalising the diagnostic reasoning we use at the bedside every day to make better, more explicit decisions under pressure.
